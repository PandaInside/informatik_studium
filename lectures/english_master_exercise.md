#### Final
First of all we have to understand what learning is. Instead of the system "education", learning is a deeply human skill: the ability to acquire knowledge, motivational when it works and different for everyone. In the TED Talk video "Is AI making us dumber?" one of the key statements was that the best known model for individual learning is one teacher for one student. But isn't it way more efficient to replace that teacher with AI?

On one hand, AI offers flexibility that no human teacher can match. It's available at any time, in any location as well as endlessly patient. On the other hand, it cannot replace the spontaneous exchange with someone who has lived their subject. A teacher brings proven expertise and human intuition. An AI brings probabilities.

But where should we draw the line between supportive usage and abandoning independent thought?

As Charlie Gedeon puts it in the video mentioned above: "The next step is an army of AIs with an army of students." That vision is not without merit. Each AI model specializes in a specific task: planning (Opus), coding (Sonnet), writing (Haiku) and so on. The multifaceted use of these various AI models could push students to actively engage, because they would need to review, adjust and connect the outputs themselves. In doing so, they would have to deal with the actual topic.

Accordingly, AI should support your thinking, not replace it. The moment you stop understanding what the tool produces and simply trust its output, you have crossed the line. Researchers call this cognitive offloading — and the real danger is not that AI gives wrong answers, but that it makes us stop asking questions altogether.

Advait Sarkar once warned that the move to autopilot is "an even greater challenge than the more commonly discussed issue of AI hallucinations or factual errors [...] the more pernicious outcome is that generative AI becomes complicit in intellectual deskilling and the atrophy of human critical thinking faculties."

A hybrid approach is the only realistic answer. We don't need to avoid AI usage, but we have to use it consciously. Personally, I use AI for knowledge transfer: I ask questions, bring up ideas, discuss them. Independent thinking and creativity should never be outsourced, because that is ultimately what defines us as human beings.

At DataShield, this principle shapes how we work. We use AI to process data faster and flag anomalies — but every finding is reviewed by someone who understands why it matters. In cybersecurity, blind trust is always a vulnerability. The same is true in learning.
